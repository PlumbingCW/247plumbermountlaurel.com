A sewer camera inspection replaces guessing with video

A sewer camera inspection is a look inside the drain and sewer lateral using a small camera, a light and a push rod. The plumber sees cracks, roots, sags and joints instead of guessing from symptoms. It is planned work, not an emergency, though it is often the right next step after a backup. You choose a time, and the plumber runs the camera through a cleanout or other access point.

Who tends to need a camera look

Buyers before closing

A home inspector usually cannot see the buried lateral. A camera run gives you condition and defects to weigh against the price.

Owners with repeat clogs

If the same drain or main has stopped up more than once, the video shows why.

Anyone who suspects damage

Sewer smells, a soggy strip of lawn or a sinkhole in the yard are reasons to look.

Sellers getting ahead of a deal

Knowing the pipe condition before listing avoids surprises when a buyer asks.

Before a remodel

If you are adding a bath or a laundry, see the line you will connect to first.

How the visit goes from start to finish

  1. Find the access point. The plumber uses a cleanout, a pulled toilet or another opening, which is why locating it early saves time.
  2. Feed the camera. The camera head travels down the pipe on a rod while you or the plumber watch a screen and a recorder captures the run.
  3. Track the depth and distance. A locator at the surface, with a transmitter in the camera head, marks where the pipe runs, how deep it sits and where each defect is.
  4. Stop at problems. When the camera meets a crack, a root mass or standing water, it pauses so the defect is recorded clearly.
  5. Review the video. The plumber replays what was found and explains what each clip means in plain language.

What the camera can show

These are the findings a good inspection looks for.

  • Cracks and breaks in clay, cast iron, PVC or ABS.
  • Roots entering through joints or damage.
  • Offset or separated joints where pipe sections have shifted.
  • Bellies, which are low spots that hold water and solids.
  • Scale, grease and debris narrowing the pipe.
  • Pipe material, since clay, cast iron and plastic are all different.
  • A connection from a branch or a tap that does not belong.

Finding the line itself before you dig or build

Many owners do not know where the sewer lateral runs, only that it leaves the house somewhere. Tracing it with the camera transmitter marks the path on the ground, which helps when planning a patio, a fence or a new tree. It does not replace an 811 call, which is still the step to take before digging for utilities.

If you plan to dig anywhere near the line, call 811 first, and ask the Mount Laurel construction office whether your project needs a permit, since rules vary.

Why a camera check makes sense for Mount Laurel buyers

Housing age and lateral condition

Most of the township's housing was built after the mid 1960s, so a lateral can easily be fifty years or more old. A buyer of an older house should not assume a pipe that old is free of roots or offsets just because the house passed its general inspection. A camera run shows what the sellers may not know.

Ask the Mount Laurel MUA to confirm where your private lateral ends and the public main begins. If the video shows trouble near the street, the MUA is the next call.

What a camera cannot do

A camera cannot see through a full blockage, standing water or a packed line, so a clogged pipe may need cleaning first. It also cannot predict how long a sound pipe will last or whether roots will return. In some old pipe, the first pass may stop at a collapse or a hard offset, and the video will show only up to that point. When that happens, the plumber says so and does not guess about the rest.

Cleaning first, and pairing the camera with jetting

When the line is dirty, the plumber may clear it with a cable before the camera goes in. After that, some owners pair the inspection with hydro jetting to clean the wall and then record a second pass. The clean video is easier to read, and it shows whether the pipe is sound enough to be jetted again.

If sewage is rising right now, that is an emergency, so call the 24/7 line and see sewer line cleaning for how the main is cleared. For trouble in a single fixture or branch, drain cleaning fits better. If the video also suggests water entering from outside, leak detection can help find the source.

What a useful inspection report contains

  • The date, the address and the access point used.
  • The pipe material and approximate length inspected.
  • A recording of the run that you can keep.
  • Time stamps or distances for each defect.
  • The depth and surface location of problems found.
  • A plain statement of what could not be seen and why.
  • Repair options, if any, without pressure to choose now.

What affects the cost of a camera inspection

A short run through an easy cleanout is a quick visit. Longer or harder lines take more time. The plumber states the price of the inspection before the camera goes in.

  • Access. A cleanout speeds the job, and pulling a toilet to reach the line adds time.
  • Length of the run. A long lateral to the street takes more feed and more footage.
  • Cleaning before the camera. A dirty line may need a cable pass or jetting for the video to be useful.
  • Locating and depth marking. Tracing the line at the surface adds equipment and time.
  • Report format. A written summary with the recording is more involved than a quick verbal look.

Insurance seldom pays for an inspection by itself, so ask your agent first if a claim is involved.
