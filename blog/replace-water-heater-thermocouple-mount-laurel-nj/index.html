Water Heater Thermocouple Replacement in Mount Laurel, NJ: Diagnose First, Then Call a Plumber

A thermocouple is the small probe that tells a gas water heater its pilot is lit. This guide helps you recognize a failing one and explains why replacing it, relighting and testing for gas leaks are plumber jobs.

By the Mount Laurel 24/7 Plumbing team · Published July 22, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026.

Read this before you touch a gas water heater

If you smell gas, do not light anything and do not flip switches. Leave the building, call 911 and the gas utility from outside, and then call us. Never relight a pilot if gas was smelled, and never relight one in a tight space such as a closet or alcove where gas can pool.

This page applies only to gas heaters with a standing pilot. If your heater lights electronically, it uses a flame sensor and a control board, not a thermocouple, and the repair is different. Replacing the part, relighting the pilot and checking the joints for gas leaks are plumber jobs, so when in doubt, stop and call.

What a thermocouple does and why it quits

A thermocouple is a short metal probe that sits in the pilot flame. The heat makes a tiny electrical signal that holds the gas valve open. When the probe wears out or gets coated with soot, the signal drops and the valve shuts, so the pilot dies as soon as you release the pilot button.

That is why the classic symptom is a pilot that lights while you hold the button and goes out within seconds of letting go. A weak flame, a draft that blows the pilot sideways, or a probe that has bent out of the flame can cause the same behavior. Replacing the part fixes it only when the probe itself is the weak link.

What a plumber does when the pilot will not stay lit

This is the sequence on a typical standing pilot heater. It is here so you know what to expect, not so you can follow it yourself.

  1. The plumber shuts the gas and lets the unit cool. The gas valve on the supply pipe is closed first, and the plumber waits so the burner area is cool and any gas has cleared.
  2. The plumber looks at the pilot assembly. With the access panel off, the plumber checks the flame shape, soot on the probe, the pilot tube and whether the probe still sits in the flame.
  3. The probe is tested before it is condemned. A meter reading on the thermocouple tells whether it still makes enough signal. Many pilot problems are drafts or a dirty pilot tube instead.
  4. The part is matched and replaced. Length, tip style and the nut thread must fit that heater. The plumber seats the nut snugly without overtightening the soft fitting.
  5. The connection is tested for gas leaks. The plumber opens the gas and brushes soapy water on the joint. Growing bubbles mean a leak, and that is the test that makes this a trade job and not a hobby.
  6. The pilot is relit and the burner and venting are checked. The plumber follows the heater label, watches the pilot hold, and looks at the burner flame and the vent draft so exhaust leaves the house.

What you can safely do before the plumber arrives

None of this involves touching gas parts.

  • Write down the model and ignition type from the label on the heater.
  • Note whether the pilot lit and then died when you let go, or never lit at all.
  • Look for rust streaks, puddles or soot on the tank, and tell the dispatcher what you see.
  • Keep the area around the heater clear of boxes, paint and anything flammable.
  • Leave the gas valve and the control knob alone if you smell even a faint odor, and go outside to make the calls.

Do not relight the pilot yourself if it keeps going out. A pilot that will not stay lit is the heater telling you something is off.

Why many Mount Laurel water heaters have already been replaced once

Local note

Most of the township's housing was built after the mid 1960s, and the population jumped from 5,249 in 1960 to 30,270 in 1990. A heater from a home's original build has often been replaced, so the unit in front of you may be much younger than the house.

That changes the decision. If your tank is young and the label shows it has not had other trouble, a thermocouple swap is a sensible repair. If the tank is old or rusty at the base, putting a new part into it is usually a poor trade, and a water heater replacement is the better investment.

Repair or replace, the honest comparison

A thermocouple is a small part, and when it is the only fault the repair is worth doing on a heater that is otherwise healthy. Look for rust streaks at the base, water around the bottom, a rumbling sound during heating, or a heater that keeps losing its pilot. Any of those suggests the tank is near the end.

If you want a plumber to confirm what is wrong before you buy anything, water heater repair includes testing the gas control, the venting and the flame. The same visit works for homes in Mount Holly and Evesham, so the guidance holds beyond Mount Laurel.

Signs the problem is bigger than a thermocouple

  • You smell gas at any point, even faintly.
  • The gas valve or the heater control is cracked, stuck or leaking.
  • The tank has rust streaks, puddles or a corroded fitting.
  • The flame is yellow, tall or uneven, or there is soot on the draft hood.
  • A replaced thermocouple fails within days.
  • The pilot assembly is hard to see or reach.

A plumber can check combustion and venting. That check matters because a heater that vents poorly can push carbon monoxide into the house.

Thermocouple questions we hear from gas heater owners

If the pilot lights but will not stay lit after you release the button, the thermocouple is the first suspect. A plumber can test it with a meter. A pilot that will not light at all points to other parts.

Replacing the part, relighting the pilot and the soapy water gas test are plumber jobs. A small mistake at the gas connection can leak, and a leak you cannot smell yet is the worst kind. Call a plumber and keep your hands off the gas valve.

There is no fixed life. Drafts, soot and age all shorten it. If your heater is old, the part may be fine and the tank may be the real problem.
