What plumbing installation covers

Plumbing installation is the planned work of adding, moving or replacing fixtures, supply lines, drains and valves so they work and pass inspection. It includes a new vanity faucet, a kitchen sink, a toilet, a dishwasher hookup and the pipes behind the walls. This is scheduled work, not a leak, so we start with a plan.

Many Mount Laurel houses were built from the late 1960s through the 1990s, so older bathrooms and kitchens are common remodel targets. When a wall is open, upgrading old supply lines and shutoff valves at the same time costs far less than doing it later.

How a new bathroom or kitchen gets plumbed

The order matters, because the pipe goes in before the walls close.

  1. Plan the layout. The plumber checks where supply and drain lines can run and marks fixture positions.
  2. Rough in the pipes. Supply lines, drains and vents are run inside the walls and floor before drywall.
  3. Test before closing up. The system is pressure tested and drains are checked, so any leak shows up while the walls are still open.
  4. Pass inspection where required. The plumbing subcode official decides which inspections apply.
  5. Set the fixtures. After tile, paint and cabinets, the toilet, sink and faucets are connected and tested.

Vents and slope: why drains need both

A drain pipe must fall at a steady slope so water carries waste along instead of leaving it behind. Too flat and solids settle, and too steep can leave water behind while solids stay put. Every fixture also needs a vent, which lets air in behind the water so the trap seal holds and the fixture drains quietly.

That is why moving a toilet across a room is more than moving a pipe. The new location must still reach the drain at the right fall, with a vent within reach. If it cannot, the layout has to change, and an honest plumber will tell you so before cutting a hole in the floor.

Water supply and what hardness means for new fixtures

The township's water quality summary lists 63 to 138 ppm of total hardness across the supplies it names. The MUA and New Jersey American Water sit near 80 to 100 ppm. That level leaves scale on new faucets, showerheads and dishwasher heating parts over time. Picking fixtures with removable aerators and cleanable screens means you can maintain them instead of replacing them.

If you are running new supply lines, copper and PEX are both common and each has strengths, so ask the plumber which suits the wall. For a whole house of aging pipe, our repiping page explains when to replace instead of patch.

What a clear written quote should list

A quote you can compare line by line is the best defense against surprises.

  • Each fixture or appliance named by type, with who supplies it.
  • The pipe material for supply and drain, such as copper, PEX or PVC.
  • Any wall, floor or ceiling opening and who repairs the finish.
  • Permit and inspection responsibility, so nobody assumes the other side handles it.
  • Shutoff valves and supply lines included or priced separately.
  • Disposal of old fixtures and cleanup.
  • What happens if a hidden problem turns up, such as rotted subfloor.

Working alongside other trades

Plumbing sits in the middle of a remodel. The rough-in goes in before insulation and drywall, and the fixtures go in after tile, flooring and cabinets. If a plumber arrives too early or too late, someone ends up cutting finished work.

Tell your contractor which day the walls close and your plumber will schedule the rough-in test before it. Tub and shower valves need blocking behind the wall, and an electrician needs to know where a disposal or a tankless unit will draw power. For hot water changes during a remodel, read about water heater installation.

What to do before any digging: call 811

A new outdoor spigot run, a water line extension or a sewer tie-in may involve digging. Call 811 before digging so buried utilities are marked. Hitting a line costs far more than waiting for the marks.

What drives the price of new plumbing

No two installs cost the same, because walls, floors and fixtures vary so much. The cost follows the amount of pipe, the access and the finish work.

  • Scope of the job. Swapping a faucet is quick, while adding a bathroom means rough-in, vents and testing.
  • Access to the pipes. Open walls and a basement below make runs easier than a slab or a finished ceiling.
  • Fixtures and materials. Higher-end fixtures and copper cost more than basic fixtures and PEX.
  • Code and inspection. Permits and inspections add steps and scheduling time.
  • Hidden conditions. Rot, old galvanized lines or undersized drains can change the plan once a wall is open.

Insurance usually does not pay for planned improvements, though a covered loss like water damage may include related repairs, so ask your agent.
