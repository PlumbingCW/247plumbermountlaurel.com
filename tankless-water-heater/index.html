What a tankless water heater is and who it suits

A tankless water heater heats water only when a tap opens, using a gas burner or electric elements to warm the flow as it passes through a heat exchanger. There is no stored supply, so it cannot run out the way a tank does, but it can only heat so many gallons per minute. Service covers installing, repairing, flushing and descaling these units.

It suits a household that wants floor space back and a steady supply of hot water for back to back showers, as long as the gas line, vent and budget match the unit. It is rarely the cheapest way to fix a bad tank, and the sections below explain why.

Running out of hot water, high gas bills and cold bursts, the symptoms that lead to tankless

Most people reach this page after an experience rather than a product search. The tank runs out halfway through the second shower. The utility closet takes up a corner of the basement you want back. The gas bill climbs every winter while the tank keeps its water hot all night for nobody. Or you already own a tankless unit and it delivers a burst of cold water in the middle of a shower.

Each of those is a different problem with a different answer. A tank that runs out may just be too small or choked with sediment, and a bigger tank or a new element can fix it for less. A unit that cannot keep up, uses more fuel than you expected or turns cold mid-use is where a tankless design, or a repair of the one you have, earns its place.

Sorting your hot water complaint

Match what you notice to the most likely cause before you spend money.

  • Hot water runs out when two showers or a shower and the dishwasher run at once.
  • Water stays lukewarm even with the tap fully on and the setting high.
  • A burst of cold water arrives in the middle of a hot shower, then heat returns.
  • The unit shows an error code or shuts off after a minute or two.
  • Flow at the tap is weaker than it used to be, with scale flakes in the aerator.
  • A clicking, rumbling or kettle sound comes from the unit as it fires.
  • Water drips from the unit or its connections, which needs a plumber to look.

The cold sandwich and how to reduce it

The cold sandwich is the slug of cold water that arrives when you pause a shower and restart it. The pipe still holds hot water from the first use, then the unit has to fire again and the cold water already in the line gets pushed through before new heat arrives. Short pauses, low flows and long pipe runs make it worse.

Fixes include keeping the flow high enough for the sensor to sustain the burner, adding a small buffer tank, installing a recirculation line, or shortening the distance to the main bath. A plumber looks at the specific unit and layout before recommending which of these helps.

Gas line and venting upgrades the swap often needs

A gas tankless burner can demand several times the fuel input of a tank burner. The existing gas line, sized for a 40 gallon tank, may be too small to feed it at full fire, which shows up as a unit that shuts down on a cold morning. Upsizing the pipe from the meter to the heater is the most common hidden cost of the switch.

Venting changes too. A tank uses a natural draft flue, while a tankless unit uses sealed plastic or stainless vent pipe run through a wall or the roof. Ask the Mount Laurel construction office about permits, and expect the plumber to confirm that the vent length and termination match the model. If a gas smell appears at any point, leave, call 911 and the gas utility from outside, then call our 24/7 line.

Why descaling matters in moderately hard water

The township's water quality summary lists 63 to 138 ppm across the supplies it names. The MUA and New Jersey American Water sit near 80 to 100 ppm. That is moderately hard to hard at the top end. A tankless heat exchanger is a narrow passage with very hot metal surfaces, which is the ideal place for scale to build. Scale cuts flow, lowers efficiency and can trigger error codes or overheating.

Scale builds inside the narrow heat exchanger of a tankless unit faster than it does in a tank. If you have no softener, treat descaling as routine, not as an extra, and ask the plumber about adding a scale inhibitor or a softener on the cold line.

Flushing a tankless heater step by step

The plumber follows the maker's procedure, and this is the usual shape of it.

  1. Isolate the unit. Power and gas are shut off, and the isolation valves on the hot and cold lines are closed.
  2. Connect the hoses. Service ports are opened and hoses attached to a pump and a bucket of descaling solution, using a product the maker approves, which is sometimes food grade vinegar. Descaling goes through the isolation valves and follows the manufacturer's instructions for that model.
  3. Circulate. The solution runs through the heat exchanger for the time in the manual, typically about an hour.
  4. Rinse and clean the filter. The unit is flushed with clean water and the inlet screen is cleaned of scale and debris.
  5. Reopen and test. Valves, gas and power are restored, and the unit is fired and checked for flow and error codes.

Flow limits and where tankless disappoints

Every unit has a maximum flow at a given temperature rise. In winter, when incoming water is colder, the same unit delivers fewer gallons per minute than in summer. If three fixtures run at once, a smaller unit will cool the stream or limit the flow.

Low flow fixtures can also be too gentle to switch some units on, leaving the water cold. Sizing starts with a count of fixtures and their flow. A plumber who recommends a tank over a tankless unit for a small, low-use house is giving honest advice, not missing a sale. For a leak or failure on an older unit, see water heater repair.

Getting a clear price for tankless work

Tankless projects vary more than tank swaps, so a firm number comes after a site look. The main drivers are gas line size, vent route, condensate and power, and removal of the old tank.

  • Unit type and capacity. Condensing, high output and electric models are priced differently.
  • Gas and electric supply. A bigger gas line or a new circuit adds work.
  • Vent and drain. The route, the material and the condensate line all affect the job.
  • Service on an existing unit. A flush, a repair or a part swap is much smaller than a full install.
  • Access. Where the unit sits, and how hard it is to reach, changes the time.

A tankless unit that fails from scale buildup is a wear item, and insurance rarely pays for wear, so a flush schedule protects you more than a claim would.
