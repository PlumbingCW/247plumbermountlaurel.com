Basement Waterproofing in Mount Laurel, NJ

Water gets into a basement through a handful of predictable paths. Finding yours decides what to fix.

Starting with where the water comes in

Basement waterproofing is the work of keeping groundwater and surface runoff out of a basement, or of collecting what gets in and sending it away. The plumbing side of that work is interior drainage and sump pumps, plus the connections that carry roof and floor water out. A wet floor that is only damp after rain is a different job from standing water after a storm.

If water is rising in the basement right now, treat it as an emergency, keep clear of electrical panels and call the 24/7 line, which a dispatcher answers on nights, weekends and holidays.

Five common ways water gets in

Cracks in the floor.

Water under the slab pushes up through hairline cracks and the joint where floor meets wall.

The wall joint.

The seam along the foot of the wall, called the cove joint, often weeps first because it sits at the lowest point.

Window wells.

A well with no drain or a clogged one fills with rain and spills over the sill.

A sump that cannot keep up.

A worn pump, a stuck float or a discharge line that freezes or clogs lets the pit overflow.

Gutters, downspouts and grading.

Downspouts dumping at the foundation and soil sloping toward the house send roof water straight to the wall.

What the 2011 storm showed about power and pumps

Local note

When Tropical Storm Irene hit in late August 2011, water backed up onto streets and into basements near the north branch of Pennsauken Creek. Ramblewood and Laurelwood were among the sections affected. Outages also lingered in Canterbury, Ramblewood, Masonville, Rancocas Woods and Fellowship, and the municipal utilities authority ran generators to keep sewage pumps going.

The practical lesson is that a sump pump runs on electricity, so the storm that fills the pit may also take out the power that empties it. A battery backup pump on its own circuit protects the basement when the grid fails.

How an interior drain and sump system works

  1. Open a channel. A strip of the floor along the wall is cut and removed so a drain can sit alongside the footing.
  2. Lay drain tile. Perforated pipe in gravel collects water under and beside the slab and slopes to the pit.
  3. Set the pit. A sump basin goes in at the low point, with a pump, a check valve to stop backflow and a float switch.
  4. Run the discharge. A pipe carries water well away from the house, and it should not ice up near the outlet.
  5. Close and test. The floor is patched with concrete, and the pit is filled with water to test the pump and the float.

Damp air, musty smell and mold: keeping it sensible

Humidity is not always a leak, and mold advice is best kept cautious.

  • Run a dehumidifier in the basement and empty it often.
  • Watch for white mineral deposits on walls, which show moisture moving through masonry.
  • Dry any wet items within a day or two, and discard soaked carpet or cardboard.
  • Do not paint over damp walls, since the paint peels and hides the source.
  • If a musty smell stays after the area is dry, have the source checked.
  • Large or recurring mold is a job for a qualified remediator, and the plumber's role is stopping the water.

What moves the cost of a dry basement project

A small sump pump replacement and a full perimeter drain are very different jobs, and the quote should say which one you are getting.

  • Length of drain needed. A single wet wall is a short run, while a full perimeter is much longer.
  • Floor type and finish. Bare concrete is simple, but a finished basement adds removal and rebuilding.
  • Pump and backup. A basic pump costs less than a pump with a battery backup or a water-powered backup.
  • Discharge route. A short run to a safe spot is easy, while a long run needs more pipe and planning.
  • Condition of the walls. Cracks and bowing may need work from another trade.

Homeowners insurance policies often treat sudden and accidental damage differently from gradual leaks or seepage, and many exclude groundwater and sewer backup unless an endorsement is added. Read your policy or ask your agent before assuming.

A walk around the house before you call

Stand outside during the next rain and look at where the downspouts end and which way the ground slopes. Clear gutters, extend each downspout several feet from the foundation, and check that window wells have drains. These small fixes cost little and sometimes end the problem.

If the basement still takes water, start with a sump pump check, which is the plumbing piece most likely to fail. For sewage coming up from a floor drain, drain cleaning or a sewer line repair is the right path instead, and a leak inside the walls belongs to a leak search.
