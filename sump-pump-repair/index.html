What a failing pump looks and sounds like

Any one of these means the pump should be checked before the next storm.

  • The pump runs constantly, even in dry weather.
  • It hums but does not move water.
  • It never starts, even when the pit is full.
  • Grinding, rattling or a loud buzz while it runs.
  • The float arm sticks against the side of the pit.
  • Water is backing up in the pit from the discharge pipe.
  • The outside end of the discharge line is frozen or blocked.
  • A musty smell rises from the pit lid.

Why power loss is the real risk here

When Irene arrived in late August 2011, water backed up into basements along the north branch of Pennsauken Creek, with Ramblewood and Laurelwood among the sections where water backed up. Outages lingered in Canterbury, Ramblewood, Masonville, Rancocas Woods and Fellowship, and the sewer authority ran generators to keep its own pumps going. An electric sump pump stops the same way when the lights go out, so a battery backup suits any basement in a flood-prone block.

A pump that is ten or more years old can fail without warning even in a mild storm.

A pump past its useful life usually costs more in repeat visits than in a new unit. If the motor is original and noisy, replacement beats another repair.

How to test your pump without waiting for a storm

Do this once or twice a year, and always before heavy rain is forecast.

  1. Check power. Confirm the cord is plugged into a working outlet, ideally a protected one.
  2. Fill the pit slowly. Pour a few buckets of water into the pit until the float lifts the pump.
  3. Watch the cycle. The pump should start, empty the pit and shut off within a short time.
  4. Listen and look. A rattle, a shaking discharge pipe or water dripping back mean something is wrong.
  5. Check outside. Make sure water is leaving through the discharge pipe and flowing away from the house.

Where the discharge pipe should and should not go

The discharge line should carry water outside, away from the foundation, onto a lawn or into a proper drain. It should never be connected to the sanitary sewer, because storm flow can overload the sewer and is not allowed in many places. If you are unsure where yours goes, the plumber can trace it.

In winter, a discharge that ends too close to the house can freeze and send water back to the pit. A longer line or a freeze-resistant outlet fitting helps avoid that.

Putting in a new pump from scratch

If your basement has no pit at all, a new installation starts with cutting the slab, digging the pit and setting a basin. The plumber then fits the pump, check valve and discharge line and tests the whole system before leaving. Ask the Mount Laurel construction office before you plan the discharge work.

A new pump is often paired with other work, such as plumbing installation for a finished basement, or a leak detection visit if water has an unknown source.

What drives the cost of a pump job

A float swap and a full new pit are very different jobs, so any number given without seeing the pit would be a guess.

  • Repair or replace. A part is cheaper than a pump, and a pump is cheaper than a new pit.
  • Pump type. Pedestal, submersible and backup units differ in price and labor.
  • Discharge routing. A short run out the wall is quick, while a long line or one under a patio takes more time.
  • Floor work. Cutting concrete for a new pit adds labor and cleanup.
  • Water already present. A flooded pit must be pumped out before work can start.

Homeowners policies often treat sudden and accidental damage differently from gradual leaks or wear, and some exclude sump pump failure unless an endorsement was added. Read your policy or ask your insurance agent before assuming.

Closing the loop before the next storm

Keep the pit clear of gravel and debris, clean the pump inlet screen if it has one, and test the battery every few months. If your block sees storm runoff, ask the plumber about an alarm that sounds when the pit fills too high. For a basement with a recurring water problem, a steady gutter and grading fix may do more than any pump, and a nearby town such as Medford gets the same advice.
