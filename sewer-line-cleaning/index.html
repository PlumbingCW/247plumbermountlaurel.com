Sewer line cleaning clears the pipe that leaves your house

Sewer line cleaning is the removal of roots, paper, grease and debris from the large pipe that carries all of your household wastewater to the street connection. It uses a heavy cable machine with cutter heads, sometimes followed by water jetting. The line is staffed nights, weekends and holidays, and the steps below will protect your floors until the plumber arrives.

Sewage coming up in the basement or first floor

A blocked main sends wastewater to the lowest opening, and that can fill a shower stall or a floor drain in minutes. Treat the water as contaminated. This is the order we suggest while you wait for the plumber.

  1. Stop every water source. Pause laundry, dishwasher, showers and flushing. The water has nowhere else to go.
  2. Keep out of the standing water. Sewage carries bacteria. Use boots and gloves if you must cross it, and keep children and pets in another room.
  3. Kill power in wet areas. If water reaches outlets, a furnace or a freezer, shut off that breaker from a dry spot, or call the electric utility if the panel is wet.
  4. Find the cleanout cap. It is usually a threaded plug on a pipe near the foundation or on the basement wall. Leave it closed until the plumber says to open it.
  5. Ask neighbors and report. If the street has the same trouble, tell the Mount Laurel MUA, since the blockage may be in the public main.

Take photos of the water line on the walls and floors before cleanup, which helps if you later make an insurance claim.

Why the first-floor shower or basement drain gives out first

Drains work by gravity, so the lowest opening on the system is the first place a blockage downstream will push wastewater out. In a house with a basement, that is a floor drain, a laundry tub or a basement bath. In a slab house, it is the first-floor tub or shower.

That is why a homeowner can flush upstairs and watch the basement shower fill. The cure is not at the shower. It is further down, past the point where the branches meet.

What the plumber does at the cleanout

Main line cleaning follows a set path. Here is what to expect on the visit.

  1. Open the cleanout and test the flow. The plumber checks whether the water level drops and which side of the cleanout holds the blockage.
  2. Run the cable. A heavy cable with a cutter head is fed down the line until it meets the blockage. Cutter shapes are chosen for roots, grease or paper.
  3. Work the blockage in passes. The cable is pushed and pulled until the line flows. Larger roots may need several cutter sizes.
  4. Flush and verify. Water is run to confirm the whole house drains. Some jobs finish with a pass with a water jetter to clean the wall.
  5. Camera the line. A short inspection shows what caused the stoppage and what condition the pipe is in.

Why a cleaned line backs up again

Roots are the most common reason a main line stoppage returns. They enter through a joint or a hairline crack, thicken as they find water, and trap paper until the line is full. Cutting clears the plug, but the opening they used is still there, so the roots grow back.

Other causes include a belly in the pipe, grease sticking to a cast iron section and flushable wipes that catch on ragged edges. If your cleaning needs to be repeated within a year, ask about a camera look and read about how a damaged line is repaired.

A look inside the pipe once the water drains

After the line flows, a camera passed through the same cleanout shows the roots, the cracks and any bellies. The recording is the best way to decide whether the cleaning was enough, and it gives you something to share with a seller or buyer. A planned version of this check is described under sewer camera inspection.

If a second fixture elsewhere is still sluggish, the blockage may be in a branch, which falls under drain cleaning. A wet patch in the yard above the line, with no rain, is a reason to also look at your water service line.

What moves the price of main line cleaning

A main line job is bigger than a sink clog, and the cost depends on the line. Ask for the price before the cable goes in.

  • Distance to the blockage. A short run to a cleanout is simpler than a long line out toward the street.
  • Roots or paper. Roots take more passes and heavier cutters than a soft plug.
  • Cleanout access. A cleanout near the foundation saves time, while no cleanout means pulling a toilet or opening a vent.
  • Camera and jetting add-ons. A camera look and a jetting pass each add time, and they often prevent a repeat visit.
  • Time of the call. Late night and holiday calls cost differently from a scheduled visit.

Insurance often treats this unevenly. On the insurance side, sewer backup coverage is often a separate add-on, so ask your agent whether yours has it before assuming.
